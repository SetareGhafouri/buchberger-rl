# Mathematical background

This note summarises the mathematics behind the repository: Gröbner bases and
Buchberger's algorithm, the freedom in the algorithm that this project
exploits, and the reinforcement-learning formulation used to learn how to
exercise that freedom. It is a condensed and revised version of Chapters 2–4
of the accompanying B.Sc. thesis (Isfahan University of Technology, 2024,
in Persian).

---

## 1. Gröbner bases

Let $R = K[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ with $K$ a field; in this project $K = \mathbb{F}_p$
with $p = 32003$. A **monomial order** $\succ$ is a total order on monomials
$x^\alpha$ that is a well-order and is compatible with multiplication
($x^\alpha \succ x^\beta \Rightarrow x^{\alpha+\gamma} \succ x^{\beta+\gamma}$).
The orders implemented are lex, graded lex and graded reverse lex (grevlex,
used in all experiments).

Every non-zero $f \in R$ has a leading monomial $\mathrm{LM}(f)$, coefficient
$\mathrm{LC}(f)$ and term $\mathrm{LT}(f)$. For an ideal $I$, let
$\langle \mathrm{LT}(I) \rangle$ be the ideal generated by all leading terms of
elements of $I$.

**Definition.** A finite set $G \subset I$ is a *Gröbner basis* of $I$ if
$\langle \mathrm{LT}(g) : g \in G \rangle = \langle \mathrm{LT}(I) \rangle$.

By Dickson's lemma every monomial ideal is finitely generated, so Gröbner
bases exist (and Hilbert's basis theorem follows). Their defining property
makes the multivariate division algorithm well behaved: the remainder of $f$
on division by a Gröbner basis is unique, and $f \in I$ if and only if that
remainder is zero. This single fact underlies the many uses of Gröbner bases —
ideal membership, elimination, solving polynomial systems, computing in
quotient rings $R/I$ (whose standard monomials form a $K$-basis, by Macaulay's
theorem), and deciding whether $\dim R/I < \infty$.

Every ideal has a unique **reduced** Gröbner basis (monic, and no term of any
element divisible by the leading monomial of another). Uniqueness is used
throughout the test suite: whatever order the S-pairs are processed in, the
reduced basis must come out identical.

## 2. Buchberger's algorithm

For non-zero $f, g$ with $L = \mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{LM}(f), \mathrm{LM}(g))$, the
**S-polynomial** is

$$
S(f,g) \;=\; \frac{L}{\mathrm{LT}(f)}\, f \;-\; \frac{L}{\mathrm{LT}(g)}\, g ,
$$

constructed so that the leading terms cancel.

**Buchberger's criterion.** $G$ is a Gröbner basis of $\langle G \rangle$ if and
only if $S(g_i, g_j)$ reduces to zero modulo $G$ for every pair $i \ne j$.

This yields the algorithm:

```
G ← F;  P ← {(f, g) : f ≠ g ∈ G}
while P ≠ ∅:
    choose (f, g) ∈ P;  P ← P \ {(f, g)}         ← the selection step
    h ← remainder of S(f, g) on division by G
    if h ≠ 0:
        P ← P ∪ {(h, g) : g ∈ G};  G ← G ∪ {h}
return G
```

Termination follows because each non-zero remainder strictly enlarges
$\langle \mathrm{LT}(G) \rangle$ and $R$ is Noetherian. The cost, however, can be
enormous: the degrees in a reduced Gröbner basis can be doubly exponential in
the number of variables (Mayr & Meyer, 1982; Dubé, 1990).

### 2.1 Where the algorithm leaves room for choice

The selection step does not affect correctness — any order yields a Gröbner
basis — but it has a large effect on cost, because the polynomials added early
determine how much work every later reduction requires. Classical heuristics:

| Strategy | Rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| First | FIFO queue order | — |
| Degree | minimise $\deg \mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{LM} f, \mathrm{LM} g)$ | — |
| Normal | minimise $\mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{LM} f, \mathrm{LM} g)$ in $\succ$ | Buchberger (1985) |
| Sugar | minimise the sugar degree of $S(f,g)$, the degree it would have in the homogenised computation | Giovini et al. (1991) |

These rules were designed from intuition and tuned on small benchmark
collections. Whether they are close to optimal on a *given distribution* of
ideals is an empirical question — the question this project asks.

### 2.2 Eliminating useless pairs

Many pairs have S-polynomials that are known in advance to reduce to zero.
The implementation uses the **Gebauer–Möller** criteria (Gebauer & Möller,
1988), in the formulation of Becker & Weispfenning (1993, §5.5):

* **Criterion B (product criterion):** if $\mathrm{LM}(f)$ and $\mathrm{LM}(g)$
  are coprime, $S(f,g)$ reduces to zero.
* **Criteria M and F (chain criterion):** when $h$ is added, a new pair
  $(g,h)$ is redundant if the lcm of another new pair properly divides its
  lcm (M), and among new pairs with equal lcm only one is kept (F); an old pair
  $(a,b)$ is redundant if $\mathrm{LM}(h) \mid \mathrm{lcm}(a,b)$ while both
  $\mathrm{lcm}(a,h)$ and $\mathrm{lcm}(b,h)$ differ from $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)$.

Elimination is applied identically for every strategy, so the learned agent
only competes on the choice that elimination leaves open.

### 2.3 Measuring cost

Wall-clock time depends on hardware and on low-level engineering. Following
Peifer et al. (2020), cost is instead the number of **polynomial additions**:
each reduction step $p \leftarrow p - c\,x^m g$ counts once, as does the
subtraction forming the S-polynomial. This is deterministic, hardware-free,
and tracks the dominant cost of a real implementation.

## 3. Pair selection as a Markov decision process

| MDP component | In Buchberger's algorithm |
|---|---|
| state $s_t$ | current basis and pair set $(G_t, P_t)$ |
| action $a_t$ | a pair in $P_t$ (the action set changes size every step) |
| transition | deterministic: reduce $S(a_t)$, update $(G,P)$ with Gebauer–Möller |
| reward $r_t$ | minus the polynomial additions spent on $a_t$ |
| initial distribution | a random distribution over ideals |

With discount $\gamma = 1$ the return of an episode is exactly minus the total
cost, so an optimal policy minimises the expected cost of a Gröbner basis
computation over the ideal distribution.

Three properties make this a demanding RL problem: the state space is
unbounded in several directions (number, length and degree of the
polynomials); the action space is dynamic; and difficulty varies by orders of
magnitude between ideals drawn from the same distribution, which makes returns
very noisy.

### 3.1 Observation and policy

To obtain a fixed-size input, the number of variables $n$ is fixed and each
polynomial is summarised by the exponent vectors of its $k$ leading terms
(zero-padded). A pair becomes a vector $x_{(f,g)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2kn}$. For
binomial ideals with $k=2$, every monomial in the computation is visible and
only coefficients are hidden.

The policy scores each pair with one shared network
$f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{2kn} \to \mathbb{R}$ and normalises over the current
pairs:

$$
\pi_\theta(a \mid s) \;=\; \frac{\exp f_\theta(x_a)}{\sum_{b\in P_t}\exp f_\theta(x_b)} .
$$

Weight sharing makes $\pi_\theta$ equivariant to permutations of the pairs and
indifferent to their number. Because each pair receives a scalar score, a
trained network can be dropped into an existing implementation as a sort key
on the pair queue. The cost of this design is that a pair is judged in
isolation, without seeing the other pairs.

### 3.2 Proximal Policy Optimization

Training uses PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). With advantage estimates $A_t$ and
probability ratio
$\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$, the
clipped surrogate loss is

$$
L(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\min\!\big(\rho_t A_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,A_t\big)\right].
$$

The clip removes any incentive to move the new policy far from the one that
collected the data; additionally, policy updates stop early once the
approximate KL divergence exceeds $1.5 \times$ a target value.

**Advantages** come from generalized advantage estimation (Schulman et al.,
2016): with $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$,

$$
A_t = \sum_{l\ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l} .
$$

**Value baseline.** As in Peifer et al., $V(s)$ is minus the cost of finishing
the computation from $s$ with the degree strategy. It is expensive (one
rollout per visited state) but informative from the first epoch; the ablation
`configs/ablation_no_value.yaml` measures its contribution.

### 3.3 The gradient, explicitly

The implementation is written in NumPy, so the gradient is derived by hand.
Let $s_j$ be the score of pair $j$ in state $t$ and $B$ the minibatch size.
The loss depends on the scores only through $\log \pi(a_t\mid s_t)$ (and the
entropy, if used). Writing

$$
g_t = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \log \pi(a_t\mid s_t)} =
\begin{cases}
-\rho_t A_t / B & \text{if the unclipped term attains the minimum,}\\
0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
$$

and using $\partial \log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)/\partial s_j = \mathbb{1}[j=a_t] - \pi_j$,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_j} = g_t\,\big(\mathbb{1}[j=a_t] - \pi_j\big).
$$

An entropy bonus $-c\,H$ with $H = -\sum_j \pi_j\log\pi_j$ adds
$(c/B)\,\pi_j(\log\pi_j + H)$. These score gradients are then propagated
through the MLP by ordinary backpropagation. All of this is verified against
central finite differences in `tests/test_gradients.py`, including the clipped
branch.

Because states have different numbers of pairs, a minibatch is stored as one
concatenated matrix with segment offsets; per-state softmax normalisation then
uses `np.maximum.reduceat` and `np.add.reduceat`, so no padding or masking is
needed.
